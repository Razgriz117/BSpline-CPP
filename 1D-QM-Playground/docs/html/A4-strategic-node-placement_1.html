Strategic Node Placement

A4 teaches the B-spline grid where the physics of the potential says the wavefunction is allowed to stop being smooth — and inserts exactly the right amount of "roughness" there, no more, no less.

1. The problem, in plain terms

This project represents the wavefunction $\psi(x)$ as a weighted sum of B-spline basis functions. The codebase's default order is $k=12$, which means every basis function is a degree-11 polynomial glued to its neighbors with $C^{10}$ continuity — ten continuous derivatives at every single grid point. That is an enormous amount of built-in smoothness, and it is silently assumed everywhere, regardless of what the potential actually looks like.

Real potentials are not that polite. A square well has a step. Two stitched-together pieces can meet with matching values but a kinked slope. A Coulomb potential blows up at the origin. At each of these points, the true $\psi(x)$ that solves the Schrödinger equation is not as smooth as the default basis assumes — one of its derivatives genuinely has a kink or a jump right there, because the physics forces it to.

If the grid doesn't know about that, the basis tries to force ten continuous derivatives through a point where the true solution only has one or two. It can't represent the kink locally, so the error doesn't stay local — it leaks into neighboring intervals as small spurious wiggles, and no amount of adding more uniformly-spaced points fully removes it, because the problem isn't resolution, it's the wrong smoothness assumption at one exact spot.

A4's job: read the potential's own piecewise definition, find every point where physics forces reduced smoothness, and tell the grid-builder exactly how much smoothness to give up there — via a decades-old B-spline trick called a degenerate knot.

2. The trick: repeating a knot lowers continuity

A B-spline grid is really a list of knots. Ordinarily each grid point (breakpoint) appears once in that list. If you instead let a breakpoint appear more than once — a "degenerate" or repeated knot — the basis functions that touch that point are forced to be less smooth there. Each extra repetition removes exactly one more continuous derivative. This isn't a hack specific to this project; it's a standard, well-understood property of the B-spline recursion, and it's exactly what A4 exploits.

For a basis of order $k$, a knot with total multiplicity $m$ gives continuity

$$C^{\,k-1-m}$$

at that point (derivatives $0$ through $k-1-m$ are continuous; derivative $k-m$ is free to jump). An ordinary grid point has $m=1$, which is where the familiar $C^{k-2}$ baseline comes from.

3. Where the "right" amount of roughness comes from

The Schrödinger equation itself pins down how a kink in $V(x)$ propagates into $\psi(x)$. The radial TISE can be written schematically as

$$\psi''(x) = 2m\bigl(V(x) - E\bigr)\,\psi(x).$$

If $V$ has a discontinuous value at a point (a step), then $\psi''$ inherits that jump directly — $\psi$ and $\psi'$ stay continuous, but the second derivative doesn't. Differentiate the equation once more and the same logic pushes a jump in $V'$ (a slope-kink, with $V$ itself still continuous) one derivative further out, into $\psi'''$. A true delta function would push the break all the way down to $\psi'$ itself. This project's plan derives that chain independently from the Schrödinger equation (the original course-note source for the table isn't in the repo), and cross-checks it against the well-known delta-potential matching condition as a sanity check.

Potential featureFirst discontinuous derivativeRequired continuityExtra knot multiplicity
Continuous join none (ordinary point) $C^{k-2}$ (the basis default) $0$
Stitched pieces, kinked slope $\psi'''$  ($n=3$) $C^{2}$  (fixed, not $k$-dependent) $k-4$
Step (value jumps) $\psi''$  ($n=2$) $C^{1}$  (fixed, not $k$-dependent) $k-3$
Delta function (cross-check only, not implemented) $\psi'$  ($n=1$) $C^{0}$  (fixed, not $k$-dependent) $k-2$
Singular potential (e.g. Coulomb $1/x$) not a continuity problem — handled by removing B-splines at the singular point (A4b), not by adding knots

Notice the required continuity column is fixed — $C^2$, $C^1$, $C^0$ — regardless of the basis order $k$. That's a statement about the physics alone: "how many derivatives of $\psi$ must stay continuous," and it doesn't care what basis represents $\psi$. What does depend on $k$ is the last column: how much multiplicity it takes to knock a basis with a much smoother default ($C^{k-2}$, e.g. $C^{10}$ at this project's $k=12$) down to that fixed physical target. That's the whole reason a high-order, very-smooth basis needs more degenerate-knot repetitions than a low-order one would — it has further to fall.

A true Dirac delta can't actually be written in this project's potential DSL (muparser evaluates ordinary pointwise expressions — there's no "infinite at a point, zero width"). It's documented as a known limitation rather than attempted; a user who wants delta-like behavior approximates it as a narrow, tall rectangular spike, and the ordinary step-detection logic already handles both edges of that spike with no extra code.

4. Turning a potential definition into join points

The potential arrives as a map<string,string> of domain → muparser expression pieces (e.g. {"[0,5)": "0", "[5,6]": "10", "(6,10]": "0"}). A4's first new function, detectPotentialStructure, walks every pair of numerically-adjacent pieces (sorted by parsed lower bound — std::map's native ordering is lexicographic on the domain string, which would silently misorder something like "[10,...)" vs. "[5,...)" if used directly) and classifies each boundary.

The detection algorithm itself, in order

  • Singularity check first. Sample each side approaching the shared boundary with a shrinking offset sequence and feed it to classifySequenceConvergence — the same numeric core A1 already built to classify domain-edge asymptotes. Divergence on either side means Singular, full stop.
  • Otherwise, finite differences at the boundary (step size $h=10^{-4}$, Richardson-extrapolated for accuracy) for both the value and the slope: a value jump beyond tolerance is Step; a slope jump beyond a looser tolerance (with no value jump) is StitchedKink; neither is Continuous (no special treatment needed).
  • Also probe each piece's own domain endpoints, not just the joins between pieces — that's what catches the single-piece Coulomb case above, which has no neighboring piece to compare against at all.

5. From join points to an actual grid

The reassuring part, confirmed by direct inspection of BSpline.cpp: its init() monotonicity check only rejects strict decreases, and its span computations already guard against zero-width spans. Repeated knots are already handled correctly by the existing math — they've just never been exercised by a non-uniform caller before. No changes to BSpline.cpp itself are needed.

6. The resultant tasks

The plan splits A4 into a required slice that fully satisfies the task's literal "done when," and a stretch slice attempted only once the required part is solid.

Detect joins, build the grid, wire it in

7. "Done when"

A potential with a domain-boundary discontinuity (the box+barrier example) produces a grid with degenerate knots exactly at the boundary, and eigenvalue accuracy measurably improves relative to a purely uniform grid at the same node count. gcovr shows all four JoinType branches actually exercised by tests.
